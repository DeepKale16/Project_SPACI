2. THE PROBLEM: THE ORBITAL DEBRIS CRISIS (The "Why")
================================================================================

Why is this project critical right now?
• Over 36,500 trackable debris objects (>10 cm) and 130 million sub-centimeter fragments 
  orbit Earth at hypervelocities between 7.5 km/s and 10 km/s.
• At 10 km/s, just 1 gram of debris carries 50,000 Joules of kinetic energy — equivalent 
  to 12 grams of exploding TNT.
• Under the NASA Standard Breakup Model (NASA-STD-8719.14C), any impact delivering over 
  40 Joules per gram of satellite mass causes catastrophic fragmentation, shattering the 
  satellite into thousands of new fragments and triggering exponential Kessler Syndrome.
• Satellites need on-board autonomous visual intelligence to identify threats in real-time, 
  distinguish operational satellites from tumbling dead space junk, and trigger Collision 
  Avoidance Maneuvers (CAM) without waiting for ground station delays.

3. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE & HOW IT WORKS (Technical Stack)
================================================================================

The SPACI 2.0 pipeline consists of four tightly coupled modular engines:

1. Deep Transfer Learning Engine (YOLO11s):
   • Trained on an audited dataset of 2,467 orbital imagery samples across 11 visual classes.
   • Upgraded from baseline YOLO11n (2.58M params) to YOLO11s (9.42M params — 3.6x capacity).
   • Trained with zero-G domain augmentations: 360-degree rotation invariance (degrees=180), 
     vertical flip (flipud=0.5), harsh solar glare jitter, and Cosine Annealing learning rate.

2. Multi-Object Kinematics & Tumble Tracker (BoT-SORT):
   • Implements temporal tracking across consecutive video frames.
   • Computes rolling variance of bounding box aspect ratios (sigma^2_AR) to measure 
     rotational tumble rates. Uncontrolled tumbling expands the collision cross-section.

3. NASA/ESA Calibrated Risk Engine:
   • Implements the continuous multi-factor collision hazard formula:
     R = 0.55 * Size_Proxy + 0.30 * Threat_Prior + 0.15 * Uncertainty + Telemetry_Adjustments
   • Maps continuous risk scores (0.0 to 1.0) into the 4 Operational States.

4. Mission Control HUD (Full-Stack Web Interface):
   • High-throughput FastAPI REST API serving semantic HTML5, Vanilla CSS glassmorphism, 
     and reactive JavaScript telemetry controls. Completely removed Streamlit to eliminate 
     latency overhead and browser reloading.

5. THE FOUR OPERATIONAL CLASSIFICATION STATES
================================================================================

1. 🚨 HAZARDOUS (Risk Score > 0.60):
   • Meaning: Object exceeds NASA catastrophic breakup threshold (40 J/g).
   • Trigger: Any object (even an active satellite) on a close, high-speed collision course.
   • Action: Autonomous Collision Avoidance Maneuver (CAM) — fire delta-v thrusters to evade.

2. 🟢 USEFUL / ACTIVE (Active Class AND Risk <= 0.60):
   • Meaning: Operational scientific payload with active station-keeping (CHEOPS, SOHO, PROBA).
   • Action: Preserve and coordinate. Maintain safety corridor and do not interfere.

3. 🔵 REUSABLE / SALVAGEABLE (Intact Defunct Bus AND Risk <= 0.40):
   • Meaning: Inactive satellite retaining structural integrity with tumble rate within robotic 
     capture limits (omega < 5 deg/s). E.g., SMART-1, Earth Observation Sat-1.
   • Action: Nominate for On-Orbit Servicing (OOS), refueling, or Active Debris Removal (ADR).

4. 🟠 INERT FRAGMENT (All other debris / moderate risk):
   • Meaning: Passive uncontrolled collision shards or space junk.
   • Action: Catalog trajectory vectors into space surveillance ephemeris database.

6. AEROSPACE FORMULAS CHEAT SHEET (For Technical Evaluators)
================================================================================

1. Kinetic Energy of Hypervelocity Impact:
   E_k = 1/2 * m_debris * v_rel^2

2. NASA Catastrophic Breakup Criterion (NASA-STD-8719.14C):
   E_p / M_target >= 40 J/g (40,000 J/kg)
   Impacts above 40 J/g completely shatter the target satellite into thousands of fragments.

3. Optical Size Proxy:
   S(r_A) = min(1.0, 3.9 * sqrt(r_A))
   where r_A = (w_bbox * h_bbox) / (W_frame * H_frame)
   Objects covering >= 6.5% of the camera FOV saturate at maximum size threat.

4. Rotational Tumble Hazard (Video Kinematics):
   T(sigma^2_AR) = min(1.0, 7.5 * sigma^2_AR)
   where sigma^2_AR is the rolling variance of the aspect ratio (width / height) over 30 frames.

5. Single-Frame Risk Formula:
   R_static = 0.55 * S(r_A) + 0.30 * P_threat + 0.15 * (1.0 - 0.25 * c) + Delta_telemetry

6. Multi-Frame Video Tracking Risk Formula:
   R_video = 0.35 * S(r_A) + 0.35 * T(sigma^2_AR) + 0.20 * P_threat + 0.10 * (1.0 - 0.25 * c) + Delta_telemetry

7. Telemetry Adjustments:
   Delta_telemetry = 0.15 * min(1.0, v_rel / 8000 m/s) + (0.06 if 400 km <= Altitude <= 900 km else 0.0)

8. TOP 10 Q&A DEFENSE (How to Answer Any Question from Evaluators)
================================================================================

Q1: "Why did you upgrade from YOLO11n to YOLO11s?"
A1: "YOLO11n has only 2.58 million parameters — it was designed for edge microcontrollers. 
    It lacked the convolutional capacity to distinguish complex satellite structures like solar 
    panels and antenna masts, causing SOHO and SMART-1 accuracy to lag. YOLO11s has 9.42M parameters 
    (3.6x capacity) with deeper C3k2 blocks and C2PSA attention. It increased precision to 87.41% 
    and brought 6 classes to 99.5% while still running in real-time at 11.5 ms (87 FPS) on our RTX 3050."

Q2: "How does the system distinguish an Active satellite from Inactive debris?"
A2: "Through two mechanisms: First, visual feature recognition identifies the operational class 
    (e.g., CHEOPS vs defunct SMART-1 bus). Second, our video kinematics tracker measures the 
    tumble rate. Active satellites use reaction wheels and thrusters to maintain 3-axis attitude 
    stabilization (tumble rate < 1 deg/s). Inactive defunct satellites tumble uncontrollably due 
    to solar radiation pressure torque (> 5 deg/s)."

Q3: "Can an active satellite ever be classified as 'Hazardous'?"
A3: "YES. If an active satellite is on a direct collision trajectory at hypervelocity, our 
    physics risk formula calculates R > 0.60. Collision avoidance supersedes object identity — 
    under NASA standards, avoiding a catastrophic impact is always the top priority."

Q4: "Why did you remove Streamlit and build a FastAPI web server?"
A4: "Streamlit re-runs the entire Python script from top to bottom on every user interaction, 
    causing high latency, UI flickering, and video streaming bottlenecks. Our FastAPI + Vanilla JS 
    architecture serves static assets via high-speed HTTP, processes REST JSON payloads in milliseconds, 
    and supports native multi-frame video streaming and Canvas overlay rendering with zero UI lag."

Q5: "How do you calculate risk if you only have a 2D camera without LiDAR?"
A5: "We use an optical angular size proxy: S(r_A) = min(1.0, 3.9 * sqrt(r_A)), derived from pinhole 
    optics where solid angle Omega = Area / Distance^2. This measures the percentage of the sensor's 
    field-of-view obstructed by the object. Combined with telemetry for velocity and altitude, 
    it provides a reliable collision risk index even on low-cost monocular space cameras."

Q6: "What is the significance of the 40 J/g NASA threshold?"
A6: "It is the standard established in the NASA Standard Breakup Model (NASA-STD-8719.14C). 
    Below 40 J/g, an impact causes localized cratering or puncture. Above 40 J/g, specific kinetic 
    energy exceeds the structural binding energy of the spacecraft, shattering it completely into 
    thousands of fragments and accelerating the Kessler Syndrome cascade."

Q7: "What augmentations did you use for training?"
A7: "Orbital space has no gravity, no horizon, and no canonical 'up' or 'down'. We trained with:
    • degrees=180.0 (full 360-degree rotational invariance)
    • flipud=0.5 and fliplr=0.5 (vertical and horizontal flip invariance)
    • hsv_v=0.4 (harsh solar glare and total space shadow contrast)
    • mosaic=1.0 and mixup=0.15 (multi-object encounter simulation)
    • close_mosaic=10 (disabled mosaic in final 10 epochs for crisp bounding box convergence)."

Q8: "What happens when a video is uploaded?"
A8: "The backend receives the video via /api/upload-video, runs BoT-SORT multi-object tracking 
    frame-by-frame, tracks bounding box aspect ratios over time to compute tumble rate variance, 
    determines velocity vectors, draws glowing HUD tracking boxes with track IDs, and exports an 
    annotated video alongside complete trajectory telemetry."

Q9: "What is the difference between Reusable/Salvageable and Useful/Active?"
A9: "Useful/Active satellites are operational assets currently performing missions — they should 
    be preserved and avoided. Reusable/Salvageable satellites are defunct, inactive spacecraft 
    (like SMART-1) that still possess intact mechanical structures and are safe to grapple (omega < 5 deg/s) 
    for On-Orbit Servicing, refueling, or controlled deorbiting."

Q10: "How fast does inference run?"
A10: "Standard inference takes 11.5 milliseconds per frame on our NVIDIA RTX 3050 GPU — approximately 
     87 frames per second. Even with multi-scale Test-Time Augmentation (TTA) enabled, it runs in 
     approximately 25 milliseconds (~40 FPS), well within real-time spaceflight requirements."
